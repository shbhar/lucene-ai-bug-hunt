HindiAnalyzer normalizes before stemming, so 20 of HindiStemmer's 67 suffix rules are lost and feminine and future verb forms keep part of their ending

The stock HindiAnalyzer folds long vowels to short ones before stemming, but the stemmer's suffixes are spelled with long vowels, so 20 of its 67 rules are lost. खाती (khātī, "she eats") becomes खात (khāt) while खाता (khātā, "he eats") becomes खा (khā), so a search for one misses the other.

The finding in 30 seconds

#

Where the expected behaviour comes from: the algorithm HindiStemmer cites (Ramanathan and Rao's light stemmer for Hindi) and Lucene's own TestHindiStemmer, which tests the stemmer filter alone; HindiAnalyzer itself documents no stem. No native-speaker check of the examples was done; each Hindi word carries a letter-by-letter transliteration.

Background: what the Hindi analyzer does

#
  1. An analyzer is a chain of filters; each filter sees the words the previous one produced. The same chain runs on documents and on queries.
  2. HindiNormalizationFilter unifies spelling variants. Among other things it turns the long vowel sign ी (ī) into the short ि (i), so खाती (khātī) becomes खाति (khāti).
  3. HindiStemFilter strips a known ending from a fixed list (-ता -tā, -ती -tī, -ते -te, -ेगा -egā, -ेगी -egī, ...) so that forms of one word share a term: खाता, खाती and खाते (khātā, khātī, khāte: "eats" for he, she, they) all become खा (khā).

The finding, step by step

#
  1. Index "वह रोज़ खाती है" (vah roz khātī hai, "she eats every day") with new HindiAnalyzer() and search for खाता (khātā, "he eats").
  2. You expect 1 hit: both words should stem to खा (khā). That is the output of the algorithm HindiStemmer cites, and Lucene's own TestHindiStemmer asserts खाती (khātī) -> खा next to खाता (khātā) -> खा.
  3. Instead you get 0 hits and no error. HindiNormalizationFilter runs first and turns the long ी (ī) into the short ि (i): खाती (khātī) becomes खाति (khāti). The stemmer has a rule for -ती (-tī) but none for -ति (-ti), so only the last ि goes: खात (khāt).
  4. The folds of ी, ू, ई, ऊ and आ (ī, ū, ī, ū, ā) cost 20 of the 67 suffix rules (5 more can never match either, but a folded twin takes over), so feminine and future forms keep part of their ending: करेगी (karegī, "she will do") -> करेग (kareg), but करेगा (karegā) -> कर (kar). On a 96-word list, 0 of 6 verb families end up with one term.
  5. Every release from 3.1.0 to 10.6.0 and main; HindiStemFilter used alone is fine. Any fix changes indexed terms, so Hindi fields need a reindex.

Who is affected

#

Every application that indexes or queries Hindi with the stock HindiAnalyzer (any constructor, every release since 3.1.0), and custom chains that normalize before stemming. Feminine habitual and many feminine and future verb forms stop matching the masculine forms; the exact form typed still matches. Missed matches, no error; likelihood plausible.

Mechanism, terms and numbers876 words

HindiAnalyzer.createComponents runs IndicNormalizationFilter -> HindiNormalizationFilter -> StopFilter -> HindiStemFilter (HindiAnalyzer.java:126-129). For every non-keyword token, HindiNormalizer rewrites the long vowel signs ी (ī) and ू (ū) to ि and ु, the long vowels आ ई ऊ (ā ī ū) to अ इ उ and the candrabindu ँ (m̐) to the anusvara ं (ṁ), and nothing writes them back (HindiNormalizer.java:57-60, :130-158). HindiStemmer, which implements Ramanathan and Rao's light stemmer, keeps its 67 suffixes in the unnormalized spelling (HindiStemmer.java:31-110): the 25 that contain one of those characters can never match inside the analyzer, and for 20 of them (-ती -tī, -ाती -ātī, -ेगी -egī, -ोगी -ogī, -ेंगी -eṁgī, -ूंगा -ūṁgā, -ाई -āī, -नी -nī, ...) no folded twin takes over. Feminine habitual and many feminine and future verb forms then keep part of their ending: खाती (khātī) -> खात (khāt) and करेगी (karegī) -> करेग (kareg), while खाता (khātā) and खाते (khāte) -> खा (khā) and करेगा (karegā) -> कर (kar), so a query for one form silently misses documents that have only the other. HindiStemFilter alone gives the bare stem, as TestHindiStemmer.testVerbs asserts. The order came with the analyzer (LUCENE-2234, 2010), so every release from 3.1.0 to 10.6.0 and main is affected. Stemming before the Hindi normalizer, with the stop step still matching normalized spelling, fixes the verbs (validated), but it splits spelling variants that share one term today (माताएँ/माताएं mātāem̐/mātāeṁ, खाती/खाति khātī/khāti), and like every fix it changes indexed terms. Low priority: missed matches only, the contract is implied, and every fix has a cost.

The bug

#
  1. HindiAnalyzer.java:126-129 runs IndicNormalizationFilter, HindiNormalizationFilter (:127), StopFilter (:128), then HindiStemFilter (:129). All three constructors share this chain (:91-108).
  2. HindiNormalizer.normalize rewrites, on every non-keyword token: ँ -> ं (m̐ -> ṁ, :57-60); आ -> अ, ई -> इ, ऊ -> उ (ā ī ū -> a i u, :131-139); ी -> ि, ू -> ु (ī -> i, ū -> u, :153-158). No rule produces any of these six characters, so after the normalizer they are gone.
  3. HindiStemmer.stem tries suffixes by length, 5 letters down to 1, each group with a minimum word length (:31-110). 25 of its 67 suffixes contain one of the six characters, so none of them can match. 5 have their folded twin in the same group, which strips the same length (ाइयाँ/ाइयां -āiyām̐/-āiyāṁ, ियाँ/ियां -iyām̐/-iyāṁ, ाँ/ां -ām̐/-āṁ, ू/ु -ū/-u, ी/ि -ī/-i). The other 20 are lost: ाएंगी -āeṁgī, ाऊंगी -āūṁgī, ाऊंगा -āūṁgā (5 letters); ाएगी -āegī, ाओगी -āogī, एंगी -eṁgī, ेंगी -eṁgī, ूंगी -ūṁgī, ूंगा -ūṁgā, ातीं -ātīṁ (4); ाईं -āīṁ, ेगी -egī, ोगी -ogī, ाती -ātī, तीं -tīṁ, ुआं -uāṁ (3); ाई -āī, नी -nī, ीं -īṁ, ती -tī (2). None of the 20 contains ँ.

Worked example

#

खाती (khātī) = ख ा त ी, 4 characters. After the normalizer: खाति (khāti). A 4-character word is too short for the 5-, 4- and 3-letter groups. The 2-letter group has ती (-tī, HindiStemmer.java:95) but not ति (-ti), so the 1-letter rule ि (-i, :109) removes one character: खात (khāt). Without the normalizer, ती matches and two characters go: खा (khā). करेगी (karegī) the same way: normalized करेगि (karegi), the 3-letter rule ेगी (-egī, :67) no longer matches, only ि goes: करेग (kareg).

The numbers

#

Through new HindiAnalyzer() on main: खाती (khātī) -> खात (khāt), करती (karatī) -> करत (karat), करेगी (karegī) -> करेग (kareg), लिखेंगी (likheṁgī, "they (f.) will write") -> लिखेंग (likheṁg), समझूंगा (samajhūṁgā, "I will understand") -> समझुंग (samajhuṁg); the masculine forms खाता (khātā), खाते (khāte) -> खा (khā), करेगा (karegā) -> कर (kar), लिखेंगे (likheṁge) -> लिख (likh). Document "वह रोज़ खाती है" (vah roz khātī hai), query खाता (khātā): 0 hits (the query parses to body:खा, the document's terms are रोज roj and खात khāt); the same document with खाता: 1 hit. On a 96-word list in 30 lemma families, 0 of the 6 verb families get one term per family today.

Related row: LFV-1388, the same shape at another site: TeluguAnalyzer folds the vowel sign ో (ō) to ొ (o) before TeluguStemFilter, so TeluguStemmer's locative -లో (-lō, "in") rule never fires. Separate site and fix.

Transliterations are letter by letter (ISO 15919 letters; only the word-final inherent a is left out, as in the diagrams), so करती is karatī (spoken kartī); anusvara ं = ṁ, candrabindu ँ = m̐.

Exposure and likelihood208 words
  • Who is hit. HindiAnalyzer through any of its three constructors, for documents and queries. Words in a stem-exclusion set are never stemmed, so they are not affected. Custom chains that copy its order (normalize, then stem) are hit the same way. HindiStemFilter used without the normalizer is not.
  • What happens. Forms of one verb get different terms: feminine habitual (-ती -tī, -ाती -ātī, -तीं -tīṁ, -ातीं -ātīṁ) and many feminine and future forms (-ेगी -egī, -ोगी -ogī, -ेंगी -eṁgī, -ूंगा -ūṁgā, -ूंगी -ūṁgī, -ाएगी -āegī, ...) keep part of their ending, so a query for one form misses documents that have only another. No exception, nothing in the logs.
  • Limits. Index and query use the same chain, so the exact form typed still matches. Several frequent masculine forms are default stop words (करना, करता, करते: karanā, karatā, karate). The corpus-level effect was not measured, and a change in which forms share a stem can affect precision as well as recall.
  • Default vs opt-in, scale. The default analyzer; no setting and no data size involved; deterministic, the same with -ea and -da.
  • After a fix. Any fix changes indexed terms, so fields analyzed with HindiAnalyzer need reindexing.

Why it looks unintended

#
  • HindiStemmer implements Ramanathan and Rao's light stemmer, whose suffix list has these endings; TestHindiStemmer.testVerbs pins खाती (khātī) -> खा (khā).
  • The chain's stop list carries normalized forms ("please add the normalized form as well", stopwords.txt:4-7); the stemmer table does not.
  • Bengali, Sorani, Persian and Arabic lose no stemmer rule to their normalizers, Telugu 3 (LFV-1388), Hindi 20 of 67.
The full argument449 words
  • The cited algorithm. HindiStemmer.java:24-25: "Implements the algorithm specified in: A Lightweight Stemmer for Hindi Ananthakrishnan Ramanathan and Durgesh D Rao." An earlier comparison with the paper's suffix list found Lucene's 67 suffixes are that list plus two (एंगी -eṁgī, एंगे -eṁge), so 19 of the 20 lost rules are the paper's own (the paper itself was not re-read for this doc).
  • Lucene's own test. TestHindiStemmer.java:56-61 (testVerbs) asserts खाना, खाता, खाती, खा (khānā, khātā, khātī, khā) -> खा, on HindiStemFilter alone.
  • The chain's stop list carries normalized forms; the stemmer table does not. The stop list has had a section "# additional normalized forms of the above" since the 2010 commit (stopwords.txt:178), and a header added in 2012 by the same author (c0319d59281, SOLR-3056) says "this file also contains forms normalized by HindiNormalizer ... When adding additional entries to this list, please add the normalized form as well" (:4-7). The stemmer table never got normalized forms. This suggests how the chain meant to treat its word lists; it is not proof.
  • A rule the same author stated. In LUCENE-1966 (#3041, 2009), on the Arabic analyzer, they wrote: "if we are going to normalize before stopfilter, I think we need to make sure the stopwords do not contain yeh without dots, or else these will not work". The stemmer's suffix list is in the same position.
  • Hindi is the largest case found. The same check on other analyzers finds no stemmer rule lost to the normalizer in Bengali, Sorani, Persian and Arabic; Telugu loses 3 of its 25 literals (the related row LFV-1388); Hindi loses 20 of 67 (a limited sweep of six analyzers).
  • The loss comes from the composition. Lucene's bundled Snowball Hindi stemmer, built from the same paper, gives खाता, खाती and खाते (khātā, khātī, khāte) one term on raw text (ख kha for all three) and splits them the same way as HindiAnalyzer once the same two normalizers run first: खाती (khātī) -> खात (khāt), apart from खाता and खाते (khātā, khāte) -> ख (kha).
  • No test covers it. TestHindiAnalyzer checks only हिन्दी/हिंदी (hindī/hiṁdī) -> हिंद (hiṁd), the exclusion set and digits; TestHindiFilters.testStemmer (:43-51) composes normalize-then-stem on one word, किताबें (kitābeṁ), which has no long-vowel suffix.
  • Precedent. Chain-composition and lost-rule defects in analyzers are fixed as bugs: #13059 ("Fix normalization in TeluguAnalyzer", missing filters, under Bug Fixes in 9.10.0) and #16972 (a BrazilianStemmer rule whose result was discarded, merged 2026-11-04).

The expected stems come from the cited algorithm and Lucene's own test; no native-speaker check was done.

The devil's-advocate check254 words

The strongest case for "intended" (argued by cross-model reviews from another model family, OpenAI GPT-5.6): HindiAnalyzer's javadoc lists HindiNormalizationFilter before HindiStemFilter (HindiAnalyzer.java:110-119); TestHindiFilters composes that order; the order, the normalizer and the stemmer came in one commit and were evaluated only end to end (FIRE 2008 MAP in LUCENE-2234); sibling analyzers also normalize first; the original author answered a sibling Indic report with "The filter here is working as documented" (#14659); and the current order has real side benefits. It gives one term to spelling variants that HindiNormalizer exists to unify ("Normalizes text to remove some differences in spelling variations", HindiNormalizer.java:24), for content words as well as stop words: माताएँ/माताएं (mātāem̐/mātāeṁ, "mothers") both -> मा (mā), खाती/खाति (khātī/khāti) both -> खात (khāt); stemming first splits them (Suggested fix). It also avoids the algorithm's own over-stemming of some nouns (पानी pānī, "water": पान pān today, पा pā with the stemmer first). No analyzer-level contract promises खाती (khātī) -> खा (khā).

Why it fails: the javadoc says which filters run, not that 20 of the bundled stemmer's 67 rules, including every feminine habitual ending, are switched off. No issue, test, CHANGES entry or commit records that as a choice. The same chain's stop list does carry normalized forms, and the Bengali, Sorani, Persian and Arabic analyzers lose no rule. The side benefits are real and shape the fix (Suggested fix); they lower severity, not validity. The contract is implied.

Suggested fix

#

Recommended A4. Stem before the Hindi normalizer; match stop words on the normalized spelling (splits spelling variants)

Recommended code fix A4: stem before the Hindi normalizer, keep the stop step matching normalized spelling (+36/-6 lines in HindiAnalyzer.java). Validated: the test passes with assertions on and off; the analysis-common suite (1910 tests) passes. Its cost: spelling variants that share one term today are split (2 of 14 probed pairs keep one). Any fix needs a reindex.

    result = new IndicNormalizationFilter(result);
    result = new NormalizedStopFilter(result, stopwords); // looks up the normalized form
    result = new HindiStemFilter(result);
    result = new HindiNormalizationFilter(result);

The future-tense gain depends on the spelling too: करूंगा (karūṁgā, "I will do") gives कर (kar) with A4, but करूँगा (karūm̐gā) still gives करुंग (karuṁg), as today. Long and short vowel variants split the same way: खाती/खाति (khātī/khāti) go from खात/खात (khāt) to खा/खात, समझूंगा/समझुंगा (samajhūṁgā/samajhuṁgā) from समझुंग/समझुंग (samajhuṁg) to समझ/समझुंग (samajh/samajhuṁg). Only the -ियाँ/-ियां (-iyām̐/-iyāṁ) plurals keep one term (2 of 14), because both spellings are in the table. That gives up, for content words, a stated purpose of HindiNormalizer ("Normalizes text to remove some differences in spelling variations", HindiNormalizer.java:24); Snowball's own Hindi stemmer design discusses the same candrabindu variation (snowball#73). How often such variants occur in real text was not measured. None of the 20 lost rules contains ँ, so this cost does not change the bug. B keeps the normalize-first order and so keeps the conflation.

Untested idea against the candrabindu part of the cost: fold only ँ to ं before the stemmer (then stem, then the full normalizer). It loses no rule, since none of the 20 lost literals contains ँ and the three that do have a ं twin; in the variant probe this emulated chain keeps all 6 candrabindu pairs together, but none of the 8 long/short vowel pairs (probe only, not run through gradle).

Shared by A4, A and B: nouns ending in -नी (-nī) or -ाई (-āī) lose more of the word, which is the cited algorithm's own output and what HindiStemFilter alone gives today: पानी (pānī, "water") -> पा (pā) instead of पान (pān), कहानी (kahānī, "story") -> कहा (kahā) instead of कहान (kahān), मिठाई (miṭhāī, "sweet") -> मिठ (miṭh) instead of मिठाइ (miṭhāi). Maintainers may want exceptions there. Every code fix changes indexed terms and needs a CHANGES note about reindexing. No option has been shown to be a net relevance win on a corpus: A4 fixes the verbs and keeps the stop handling but splits spelling variants, B keeps the variants but over-stems -ति (-ti) nouns, and C changes nothing; the choice needs a maintainer's judgement, ideally with a native speaker or a relevance test.

A4. Stem before the Hindi normalizer; match stop words on the normalized spelling (splits spelling variants) (validated by the checks) (recommended)

#

Chain: IndicNormalizationFilter -> stop step -> HindiStemFilter -> HindiNormalizationFilter. The stop step is a small private FilteringTokenFilter that looks up the HindiNormalizer form of a copy of the term and passes the term on unchanged (a keyword token is looked up as it is, Indic-normalized but not Hindi-normalized, as today). Stop-word removal is unchanged on the 225 stop words and 106 stop variants; the stemmer's input changes to the spelling its table is written in, which fixes the verbs and splits the spelling variants above. +36/-6 lines in HindiAnalyzer.java, including the javadoc.

    result = new IndicNormalizationFilter(result);
    result = new NormalizedStopFilter(result, stopwords); // looks up the normalized form
    result = new HindiStemFilter(result);
    result = new HindiNormalizationFilter(result);

Trade-offs

Splits spelling variants of content words that share one term today: माताएँ/माताएं (mātāem̐/mātāeṁ) -> माताएं/मा instead of मा/मा, खाती/खाति (khātī/khāti) -> खा/खात instead of खात/खात; 2 of 14 probed pairs keep one term (14 today). Exposes the algorithm's own -नी/-ाई (-nī/-āī) noun over-stemming (above). A new private filter of about 25 lines that needs its own tests (normalized stop lookup, a custom stop set, a stem-exclusion set, the spelling variants). Changes indexed terms: reindex.

+    result = new HindiNormalizationFilter(result);

Trade-offs

The same spelling-variant split as A4 (2 of 14 probed pairs keep one term). StopFilter then sees unnormalized text: the 225 list entries still match, but the 106 stop variants are no longer removed (काफी kāphī, अँदर am̐dar, ...). The same noun over-stemming as A4. Changes indexed terms: reindex.

What the checks validated

Same scratch worktree: the repro passes and org.apache.lucene.analysis.hi.* passes 18 of 18, with assertions on and off; checkGoogleJavaFormat passes.

B. Add the normalized twin of each lost suffix to HindiStemmer (validated by the checks)

#

Keep the chain; add ति (-ti) next to ती (-tī), ेगि (-egi) next to ेगी (-egī), and so on for the 20 lost suffixes.

Trade-offs

Keeps the normalize-first chain and the stop handling, so it keeps today's spelling-variant conflation (14 of 14 probed pairs), and fixes the verbs, but also over-stems the very common -ति (-ti) nouns, where the paper's rules remove only the final ि (-i): नीति (nīti, "policy") -> नि (ni) instead of नित (nit), स्थिति (sthiti, "situation") -> सथि (sathi), शांति (śāṁti, "peace") -> शां (śāṁ), 6 of the 7 probed. It also changes HindiStemFilter used alone. Not recommended.

What the checks validated

Measured with the probes only (the table above); not run through gradle.

Stem, normalize, then stop (validated by the checks)

#

IndicNormalizationFilter -> HindiStemFilter -> HindiNormalizationFilter -> StopFilter, an order one reviewer suggested.

Trade-offs

Rejected by measurement: the stop list then sees stems, so 91 of the 225 stop words survive and 10 of 96 content words are dropped (करेगी karegī and करेगा karegā stem to कर kar, which is a stop word); it also splits the spelling variants (2 of 14 pairs keep one term).

What the checks validated

Measured with the probe only.

C. Document the behaviour

#

State in the HindiAnalyzer javadoc that feminine and future verb forms are not conflated with the masculine ones.

Trade-offs

No reindex and no relevance change, but the 20 dead rules stay dead. Not run.

Downstream note

#

Products that wrap the stock HindiAnalyzer or copy its filter order inherit the same split stems; one large downstream deployment's published Hindi chain (Wikimedia CirrusSearch) keeps the same order.

How it was found

#

Semi-formal: a bounded metamorphic check fed every one of HindiStemmer's 67 suffixes, on a root, through the public HindiAnalyzer and through HindiStemFilter alone, and flagged where the results differ. An exhaustive classification against the normalizer's rewrites gave the same 20 lost suffixes, and it was reduced to one public-API test.

The method in full96 words

Method: semi-formal, a bounded metamorphic check (2026-11-05, main 5adff78bb168): does stemming commute with the normalizer? Each suffix literal, appended to a root, went through the public HindiAnalyzer and through HindiStemFilter alone.

  • An exhaustive classification of all 67 literals against HindiNormalizer's unconditional rewrites: 25 can never match, 5 of them have a twin in the same group that takes over, 20 are lost; no rewrite target is rewritten again, so one pass is a fixed point.
  • The witness is the one-test repro above, run on the stock main jars with assertions on and off.

LFV-1387, snapshot of 2026-11-10

Registry title
HindiAnalyzer runs HindiNormalizationFilter before HindiStemFilter, so the 20 of HindiStemmer's 67 suffix rules that are spelled with a long vowel or candrabindu (-ती -tī, -ाती -ātī, -ेगी -egī, -ोगी -ogī, -ूंगा -ūṁgā, -ाई -āī, -नी -nī, ...) can never fire, and feminine and future verb forms keep part of their suffix: 'खाती' (khātī) is indexed as 'खात' (khāt) while 'खाता' (khātā) and 'खाते' (khāte) give 'खा' (khā)
Area
analysis-common / Hindi analyzer (HindiAnalyzer filter chain; HindiStemmer suffix table versus HindiNormalizer)
Priority
Low, rank 308: In every release since 3.1.0 the stock HindiAnalyzer gives everyday feminine and future verb forms (खाती khātī, करेगी karegī, समझूंगा samajhūṁgā) a stem of their own, so a search for another form of the same verb silently misses documents that hold only these forms. It stays Low because the analyzer-level contract is implied and disputed, the exact form typed still matches, and every fix trades off some noun over-stemming and changes indexed terms.
Impact class
minor
Impact note
Missed matches only, with no error: every application that indexes or queries Hindi text through the stock HindiAnalyzer (all three constructors share the chain), on every release from 3.1.0 through 10.6.0 and on main, gets a stem of their own for feminine habitual and future verb forms whose suffix is spelled with ी (ī), ू (ū), ई (ī), ऊ (ū), आ (ā) or ँ (m̐) (खाती khātī -> खात khāt, करती karatī -> करत karat, करेगी karegī -> करेग kareg, लिखेंगी likheṁgī -> लिखेंग likheṁg, समझूंगा samajhūṁgā -> समझुंग samajhuṁg) while the masculine forms reach the bare stem (खाता khātā and खाते khāte -> खा khā, करेगा karegā -> कर kar), so a search for one form misses documents that hold only the other; HindiStemFilter used on its own is not affected, no scale or setting is needed, -ea and -da behave the same, and because any fix changes indexed terms, affected fields need reindexing after it.
Summary
You index Hindi text with the stock HindiAnalyzer; one document says 'वह रोज़ खाती है' (vah roz khātī hai, 'she eats every day') and a query says 'खाता' (khātā, the masculine form of 'eats'). You expect both forms to stem to 'खा' (khā), because HindiStemmer implements Ramanathan and Rao's light stemmer, whose suffix list contains -ती (-tī), and Lucene's own TestHindiStemmer pins खाती (khātī) -> खा next to खाता -> खा. Instead HindiAnalyzer runs HindiNormalizationFilter first, which folds the long vowel signs ी (ī) and ू (ū), the long vowels आ (ā), ई (ī) and ऊ (ū) and the candrabindu ँ (m̐) to their short or plain forms, so the 20 suffix rules spelled with those characters can never match: 'खाती' is indexed as 'खात' (khāt) and the query finds nothing, with no error.
